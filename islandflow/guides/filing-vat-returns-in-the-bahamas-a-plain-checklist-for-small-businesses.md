VAT returns have a way of sneaking up on a busy owner. One day it's the start of the period, the next you're digging through a shoebox of receipts the night before the deadline. This checklist is meant to make the job routine. It works whether you keep your books in a notebook, a spreadsheet or software.

A quick note before we start: this is general guidance, not tax advice. VAT rates, thresholds, filing frequencies and deadlines in The Bahamas have changed before and can change again. Always confirm the current rules with the Department of Inland Revenue (DIR) through its official website or offices, and speak to your accountant if your situation is unusual.

## Step 1: Know your own VAT setup

Before you can file correctly, be sure of the basics for *your* business:

- **Are you registered?** Registration depends on your turnover and circumstances. If you're unsure whether you need to register, or whether you've crossed the threshold, check with the DIR.
- **What is your filing period?** The DIR sets how often you file. Find your period on your registration documents or your account on the DIR's online system, and don't assume it's the same as a friend's business.
- **What is your due date?** Write the deadline for each period on a calendar you actually look at. Set a reminder a week before, not the day before.
- **What rates apply to what you sell?** Most goods and services carry the standard rate, but some supplies are zero-rated or exempt. Confirm the current standard rate and the treatment of your main products with the DIR rather than relying on memory or old invoices.

Write these four answers on one page and keep it with your VAT records. It saves you looking them up every period.

## Step 2: Gather your records for the period

A VAT return is only as good as the records behind it. For each period, pull together:

- **Sales records:** every invoice issued, plus till or point-of-sale summaries for cash sales.
- **Credit notes and refunds:** anything that reduced a sale after the fact.
- **Purchase invoices:** proper VAT invoices from your suppliers for goods and services you bought for the business.
- **Import documents:** customs entries showing VAT paid at the border on imported goods.
- **Bank statements:** to cross-check that sales and purchases match what actually moved through your accounts.

Keep these organised by period. A simple folder (paper or digital) for each period goes a long way. Ask the DIR how long you must keep records, and don't throw anything out early.

## Step 3: Work out your output VAT (on sales)

Output VAT is the VAT you charged customers.

1. Total your standard-rated sales for the period.
2. Separate out zero-rated and exempt sales. They are usually reported differently, so don't lump them in.
3. Subtract credit notes and refunds that relate to the period.
4. Calculate the VAT charged, or total the VAT shown on your invoices.

**Check you can run:** add up the VAT figure from your invoices one by one, then compare it to the total from your till or sales report. If the two don't match, find out why before you file. Common causes are voided sales that weren't removed, duplicate invoices, or a price entered as VAT-inclusive when it should have been exclusive.

## Step 4: Work out your input VAT (on purchases)

Input VAT is the VAT you paid on business purchases and imports, which you may be able to claim back.

1. List every purchase invoice for the period that shows VAT.
2. Include VAT paid on imports, using your customs documents.
3. Remove anything that wasn't for the business, or that the rules don't let you claim. Confirm with the DIR or your accountant which items are restricted.
4. Total the claimable input VAT.

**What to watch for:**

- **No proper invoice, no claim.** A till slip or a quote is often not enough. Make sure supplier invoices show the details the DIR requires, including the supplier's VAT registration details.
- **Mixed use.** If something is used partly for personal or exempt purposes, you may only be able to claim part of the VAT. Ask how the apportionment should work.
- **Exempt supplies.** If you make exempt sales, it can limit how much input VAT you can recover. This one is worth a conversation with your accountant.

## Step 5: Reconcile before you file

This is the step most people skip, and it's where most errors are caught. Run these checks:

- **Sales vs. bank:** do your recorded sales roughly line up with deposits for the period, allowing for timing and cash on hand?
- **Purchases vs. bank:** are the supplier invoices you're claiming backed by real payments or by amounts you owe?
- **Period cut-off:** are all invoices dated inside the period? A sale on the last day of the month belongs in that month, not the next.
- **Last period's return:** did you correct anything last time that carries into this period?
- **Unusual swings:** if your VAT payable or refund is very different from the usual, make sure there's a real business reason, such as a big equipment purchase or a slow season, and not a mistake.

## Step 6: Complete and submit the return

1. Log in to the DIR's online system, or use whatever filing method the DIR currently requires.
2. Enter your output VAT, input VAT and any other figures the form asks for, using the totals from your workings.
3. Double-check each figure against your summary sheet. Typing errors are common.
4. Submit before the deadline and save or print the confirmation.
5. Keep a copy of your workings with the confirmation in that period's folder.

## Step 7: Pay, or claim your refund

- If output VAT is more than input VAT, you owe the difference. Confirm the accepted payment methods and the payment deadline with the DIR, and pay on time. Late filing and late payment can attract penalties and interest.
- If input VAT is more than output VAT, you may be in a refund or credit position. Check the DIR's current process, and expect that larger refund claims may be reviewed, so keep your records tidy.
- File even when you have nothing to pay. A nil return is usually still a return, and missing it can still count as late filing. Confirm this with the DIR for your situation.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Filing from memory or estimates instead of actual records.
- Claiming VAT on purchases without a valid VAT invoice.
- Mixing personal and business spending in the same account.
- Treating every sale as standard-rated when some are zero-rated or exempt, or the other way round.
- Leaving the work until deadline day, when there's no time to chase a missing invoice.
- Not keeping proof that you filed and paid.

## Make it a monthly habit

The businesses that find VAT painless usually aren't doing anything clever. They just keep up as they go: entering sales and purchase invoices weekly, filing receipts the day they arrive, and reconciling to the bank at month end. By the time the return is due, the numbers are already sitting there.
