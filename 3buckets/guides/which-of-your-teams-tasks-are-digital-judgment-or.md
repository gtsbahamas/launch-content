I'm Ty Wells, founder of 3 Buckets. This guide shows you how to sort your team's work into three buckets using a spreadsheet and some honest conversations. You don't need any tool to do it.

## Start with tasks, not job titles

The most common mistake is to ask "can we automate this role?" Roles are bundles. A bookkeeper's week might include data entry, chasing a late-paying client and deciding whether an unusual expense is legitimate. Those are three different kinds of work, and they need three different answers.

So the unit you sort is the task: one repeatable piece of work with a clear start and finish. "Reconcile the bank feed" is a task. "Finance" is not.

## Three working definitions

These are the plain definitions I use in this guide. Adjust the wording to fit your organisation, but keep the distinction sharp.

- **Digital work** follows rules that could be written down. The inputs are predictable, the steps are the same each time, and a correct answer can be checked. These are your candidates for automation.
- **Judgment work** needs a person to weigh options, but parts of it can be prepared or drafted by software. A person still makes the call and owns it. These are your candidates for an AI co-pilot that supports someone, not one that replaces them.
- **Contributor work** depends on trust, relationships, context or care that people bring. Clients, colleagues or the public would notice, and mind, if it were handed to a machine. This is the work you protect.

## Step 1: List what actually gets done

Ask each person, or each team lead, to list the tasks they did in a recent normal fortnight. Not the job description, the real work. For each task, capture:

1. A short name (verb plus object: "approve supplier invoices").
2. Roughly how often it happens.
3. What goes in (emails, forms, data, conversations).
4. What comes out (a record, a decision, a reply, a relationship maintained).
5. Who would notice if it stopped.

Expect the list to be messy. People forget the small things they do on autopilot, and those small things are often the best Digital candidates. A second pass a few days later usually adds a lot.

## Step 2: Ask four questions of each task

Run every task through the same four questions. Write the answers down, because the reasoning matters more than the label.

1. **Could I write the steps down so a new starter gets the same result every time?** If yes, lean Digital.
2. **Does the task end in a decision that someone has to stand behind?** If yes, lean Judgment, even if most of the preparation is routine.
3. **Would the person on the other end care that a human did it?** If yes, lean Contributor.
4. **What happens when it goes wrong?** A wrong figure in a report can be fixed. A wrong word to a grieving customer or a struggling employee is harder to undo. The higher the cost of error, the more cautious you should be about moving the task toward automation.

Many tasks will split. "Respond to customer complaints" might be Digital for logging and routing, Judgment for deciding on a refund, and Contributor for the conversation with an upset long-standing client. When that happens, break the task into its parts and sort each part.

## Step 3: Sort, then argue

Put a provisional bucket next to each task. Then get the people who do the work, and the people who manage it, in a room and disagree productively. Useful prompts:

- "What would you be uneasy about a machine doing here?"
- "Which part of this do you dread because it's tedious, not because it's hard?"
- "Where do you use your own knowledge of the client or the history that isn't written anywhere?"

The people closest to the work usually know where the hidden judgment sits. They also know which "judgment" is really just habit.

## Checks to run before you change anything

A sort is a hypothesis. Before you automate or reassign anything, test it.

- **The exception check.** Pull the last few weeks of a supposedly Digital task and count how often someone had to step outside the normal steps. If exceptions are frequent, it may be Judgment work wearing a Digital costume.
- **The handover check.** Ask whether you could hand the task to a capable temp with written instructions. If the honest answer is "only if they knew our clients," there's Contributor work inside it.
- **The accountability check.** For every Judgment task, name the person who owns the decision once an AI co-pilot is helping. If nobody can be named, don't proceed.
- **The compliance check.** Some tasks carry legal duties around data protection, employment, record-keeping or regulated advice. These rules differ by country and sector and they change, so confirm the current requirements with the official regulator or a qualified adviser before you automate or reassign anything that touches personal data or regulated decisions.
- **The people check.** If a role is mostly Digital work, decide openly what happens to the person before you change the work. Consultation rules can apply, so confirm your obligations with the official source for your jurisdiction.

## What to watch for

- **Sorting by how the task feels.** Boring is not the same as Digital. Some dull tasks hide real decisions.
- **Overprotecting.** Calling everything Contributor work is a comfortable way to avoid change. Push for specifics: who exactly would notice, and why would they mind?
- **Underprotecting.** The tasks that build trust with clients and colleagues rarely look impressive on a list. Losing them can quietly damage the business.
- **Treating the labels as permanent.** Tools change and your business changes. A task that needs judgment today may become rule-based once you've written the rules down.
- **Counting savings you haven't seen.** Estimates of time saved are guesses until you measure the work before and after a change.

## Keep the sort alive

Put a date in the diary to revisit the list, especially after you introduce a new tool, lose or hire a key person, or change how you serve customers. Each time, ask the same four questions and rerun the checks. Over time you'll have something more useful than a one-off audit: a shared, plain-language picture of what your people do, what software should handle, and what you've decided is worth protecting.
